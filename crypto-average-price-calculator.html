    What the average buy price tells you

    If you bought the same coin several times at different prices, the number that matters is your average buy price, also called your average entry or cost basis per coin. It is the price at which the whole position breaks even. Sell above it and (ignoring fees) you are in profit; sell below it and you are in loss. Exchange apps often show this number, but it is easy to lose track of across wallets, exchanges and transfers, so a quick independent check is useful.

    The formula: a weighted average

    Averaging the prices you paid is wrong unless every buy was the same size. The correct method weights each price by how many coins you bought at it:

    • Total cost = sum of (price × quantity) for every buy
    • Total quantity = sum of the quantities
    • Average price = total cost ÷ total quantity

    Worked example

    You buy 0.1 BTC at $60,000, then 0.2 BTC at $50,000, then 0.3 BTC at $40,000. The simple average of the three prices is $50,000, but that overstates your cost because you bought the most when the price was lowest. The weighted calculation gives a total cost of $6,000 + $10,000 + $12,000 = $28,000 for 0.6 BTC, an average price of about $46,667. If Bitcoin trades at $55,000, the position is worth $33,000, a gain of $5,000 or about 17.9% on cost — even though your first purchase, at $60,000, is still under water.

    Averaging down: what it does and does not do

    Buying more after a price drop pulls your average down, which lowers the price you need to get back to break even. That is the logic behind "averaging down". It is also how a small loss becomes a large one: each extra buy increases the money exposed to the same idea. Before adding to a losing position, ask whether you would open the position today at this price with no history. If the answer is no, a lower average cost is not a reason to buy.     Fees and the real break-even price

    Every buy and sell has a fee, so the true break-even price is a little above the average buy price. A 0.1% fee on each side raises break-even by roughly 0.2%. If you want an exact figure for a single trade, use the crypto ROI calculator, which includes fees on both sides and shows the break-even sell price.

    FAQ

    Crypto average price calculator FAQ

    How do I calculate the average buy price of a coin?

    Multiply each purchase price by its quantity, add the results to get the total cost, and divide by the total number of coins. Do not simply average the prices unless every purchase was the same size.

    What does my average entry price mean?

    It is the price at which your whole position breaks even, before fees. If the market price is above it you have an unrealised gain, and if it is below you have an unrealised loss.

    Does averaging down reduce risk?

    It lowers your average price, so a smaller recovery gets you back to break even, but it also increases the amount of money exposed to the same position. It is a decision to add risk, not remove it.

    Are fees included in the average price?

    No. To include them, add the fee to each purchase price or use the ROI calculator, which handles fees on both the buy and the sell.

    Can I use this for stocks or other assets?

    Yes. The weighted-average formula is the same for any asset bought in several lots; the labels just say coins and USD.

    Is this the same as my tax cost basis?

    Not necessarily. Tax rules often require a specific lot-matching method and vary by country, so use this for your own tracking and ask a tax professional about reporting.
