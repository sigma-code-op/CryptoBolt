    How a market works: buyers, sellers and price

    By the end of this lesson: Understand where a price comes from, read a trading pair, and avoid the beginner mistake of judging a coin by how cheap it looks.

    Before you click any button, you need a clear picture of what a price actually is. It is simpler than it looks, and once you see it, charts and order screens start to make sense.

    Price is where a buyer and a seller meet

    An exchange is a meeting place. Some people want to buy and say the highest price they will pay. Others want to sell and say the lowest price they will accept. When a buyer and a seller agree, a trade happens, and that agreed price becomes the latest price you see on the screen.

    Nobody "sets" the price. It is simply the most recent deal. If more people want to buy than sell, buyers have to offer more to get filled, and the price drifts up. If more people want to sell, sellers have to accept less, and it drifts down. That is all "supply and demand" means here.

    Example: Imagine a coin where one person offers to sell at $100.10 and another offers to buy at $100.00. Nothing happens until one of them moves. If the buyer raises the bid to $100.10, a trade occurs at $100.10 and the screen shows $100.10. The list of waiting offers is the order book, which you will meet again in lesson 4.

    Reading a trading pair

    Every market is a trading pair written like BTC/USDT. The first part is the thing you are buying or selling (the base). The second part is what you pay with (the quote).

    PairBase (what you trade)Quote (the price is in this)Reads as
    BTC/USDTBitcoinUSDT, a stablecoin pegged to the US dollar"One bitcoin costs this many dollars"
    ETH/BTCEtherBitcoin"One ether costs this many bitcoin"
    SOL/USDTSolanaUSDT"One SOL costs this many dollars"

    Buying BTC/USDT means you spend USDT and receive BTC. Selling does the opposite. Beginners usually start with pairs quoted in a dollar stablecoin because the price is easy to read.

    Spot: the simplest way to trade

    Spot trading means you buy the actual asset and it lands in your account. You can only make money if the price rises after you buy. The most you can lose is what you put in. That makes spot the right place to start. Other products, like futures, add leverage and extra risks, which we cover in lesson 6.

    Why a cheap price does not mean a cheap coin

    Here is a trap that catches almost every beginner: "This coin costs $0.0004, so it has lots of room to grow, but Bitcoin costs a fortune." The price of one unit says nothing about how big or valuable the whole project is. What matters is the market cap:

    Market cap = price of one coin × number of coins in circulation.

    A coin priced at $0.0004 with a trillion coins in circulation is worth $400 million. A coin priced at $400 with 1 million coins is worth the same $400 million. The tiny price is just a result of how many units exist. A low price does not make a coin "undervalued", and it does not make it more likely to rise.

    Two words you will hear constantly

    • Liquidity is how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. Big pairs like BTC/USDT are very liquid. Tiny coins can be very hard to exit.
    • Volatility is how much and how fast the price moves. Crypto is more volatile than most traditional markets, and it trades 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

    High volatility is why people are drawn to crypto and also why it can hurt so quickly.

    Scenario: the spread is the first cost

    The best bid is $99.90 and the best ask is $100.10. The gap is $0.20, or 0.2%. If you buy at the ask and immediately sell at the bid, you lose $0.20 per coin before any fee. That is why a price on a chart is not a price you can trade at: you buy slightly above it and sell slightly below it.

    Common beginner mistakes here

    • Believing the last price is the price you will get.
    • Trading thin markets where the spread is wide.
    • Assuming a price move is news when it is only a trade hitting one side of the book.

    What to remember

    • The price on screen is simply the last deal between a buyer and a seller; more buyers push it up, more sellers push it down.
    • In BTC/USDT you trade the first asset (base) and pay in the second (quote).
    • Judge a coin by market cap, not by how cheap one unit looks. Spot trading, where you own the asset, is the safest place to start.
    Try it: In the terminal, open three different pairs. For each one write down the base, the quote and the 24-hour volume. Which pair looks the most liquid? Which looks the least?

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. In the pair ETH/BTC, what are you buying when you buy the pair?

    The first asset is the base (what you receive when you buy) and the second is the quote (what you pay with). So buying ETH/BTC gives you ether and costs bitcoin.

    2. What is the 'price' you see on a chart, in plain words?

    Price is just the latest agreed deal. The exchange does not decide it.

    3. Coin A costs $0.01 and coin B costs $500. Which one is automatically 'cheaper' to own as a company-sized bet?

    Price per unit depends on how many units exist. Market cap (price × circulating supply) is the fair way to compare size.

    4. Why is spot trading a sensible place for a beginner to start?

    On spot you buy the real asset, so your maximum loss is the amount invested. There is no borrowed money that can force you out.
