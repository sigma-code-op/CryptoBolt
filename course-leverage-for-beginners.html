    Leverage and liquidation, explained simply

    By the end of this lesson: Understand what leverage really does, calculate roughly where a leveraged position gets liquidated, and decide to treat it with great caution.

    Leverage is the single most dangerous tool a beginner can touch, and exchanges make it one click away. This lesson is long on purpose. If you remember one thing from the course, make it this lesson.

    What leverage is

    Leverage lets you open a position larger than the money you put up. You deposit your own money as margin, and the exchange lends you the rest. With 10x leverage, $100 of margin controls a $1,000 position.

    Position size = margin × leverage. At 10x, $100 margin = $1,000 position. At 50x, the same $100 controls $5,000.

    Leverage is available on products like perpetual futures, which are different from spot. If you want to see the differences, read spot vs. perpetual futures.

    It multiplies gains and losses

    The result on your position is magnified by the leverage, in both directions:

    Price movesNo leverage10x leverage
    +5%+5% on your money+50% on your margin
    -5%-5% on your money-50% on your margin
    -10%-10%About -100%: your margin is gone

    The exciting row is the first one. The row that ends accounts is the last.

    Liquidation: when the exchange closes you out

    Because the exchange lent you money, it protects itself. If the price moves against you so much that your margin is nearly used up, the exchange force-closes your position. This is liquidation. You lose the margin you put in for that position, and you do not get to choose the exit.

    A rough rule for a long position (ignoring fees and a small safety buffer called maintenance margin): the price only needs to fall about 100 divided by the leverage percent from your entry.

    LeveragePrice drop that wipes your margin (approx.)
    2xabout 50%
    5xabout 20%
    10xabout 10%
    25xabout 4%
    50xabout 2%
    100xabout 1%

    Crypto routinely moves 2% in a single hour. At 50x or 100x, ordinary noise liquidates you. This is why the most common beginner story is "I made a few trades and lost everything".

    Other dangers of leverage

    • Funding fees on perpetuals are charged while you hold, whether you are winning or not.
    • It shrinks your room for being wrong. With a tight liquidation point, you cannot survive a normal dip even if your idea turns out right later.
    • It tempts you to over-size. Leverage turns a small account into a large position, so a single mistake is huge.
    • Emotions get louder. Watching 50% swings on your margin makes clear thinking hard.

    A sensible rule for beginners

    Key idea: Start with spot and no leverage. Practise leverage only in paper trading, and keep it very low (1x to 3x) while you learn. Never use leverage to "make up" for a small account. If you cannot make money without leverage, leverage will not fix that. It will only make the losses bigger.

    Leverage in numbers, step by step

    Leverage lets a small amount of money, called margin, control a larger position. With $100 of margin at 10x, you control a $1,000 position. Gains and losses are calculated on the full $1,000. A 1% move in price is therefore a 10% move in your margin: $10 on $100.

    Now see what happens when the market turns. At 10x, a 10% move against you removes your entire margin, so the exchange closes your position. That is a liquidation, and it can happen on a perfectly normal day for many coins. At 20x the same thing happens on roughly a 5% move. At 50x, close to 2%. Fees and maintenance margin make the real distance a little shorter than these round figures.

    LeveragePrice move that wipes out margin (approx.)Loss on margin from a 2% adverse move
    2xabout 50%4%
    5xabout 20%10%
    10xabout 10%20%
    25xabout 4%50%
    50xabout 2%100%

    The table uses simple round numbers to show the shape. Your exchange's exact liquidation price depends on fees, margin mode and maintenance margin, so always check it with the {{liquidation-price-calculator|liquidation price calculator}} before you open a trade.

    The difference between leverage and risk

    Leverage and risk are related but not the same. What you actually risk on a trade is the distance from your entry to your stop, times your position size. You can trade 10x leverage and still risk only 1% of your account if the position is small and the stop is close. You can also trade 2x leverage and risk 30% of your account if the position is huge and there is no stop. The leverage number is a limit on how much room you have before liquidation, not a measure of how dangerous the trade is.

    That is why the safest way to use leverage is the opposite of how it is advertised. Decide your stop first, size the position so that hitting the stop costs about 1% of the account, then use only as much leverage as needed to open that position. If the liquidation price sits comfortably beyond your stop, you are using leverage as a tool. If your stop sits beyond your liquidation price, the exchange will close you out before your plan does.

    Isolated and cross margin, briefly

    With isolated margin, only the margin you assign to a position is at risk. If it is liquidated, the rest of your account is untouched. With cross margin, your whole balance backs the position, which gives more room before liquidation but can put the entire account at stake. Beginners are usually better served by isolated margin, because the worst case is capped and visible.

    Habits that keep leverage from hurting you

    • Never add margin to a losing position just to push the liquidation price away. It turns a small, planned loss into a large one.
    • Start at the lowest leverage that lets you open the position you planned. Raise it only when you can explain why.
    • Expect fast moves around news and weekends, and avoid maximum leverage during them.
    • Practise with leveraged paper trades first. Seeing a liquidation happen with fake money is far cheaper than with real money.

    Scenario: $500 at 5x

    You post $500 of margin at 5x leverage, so the position is worth $2,500. If price falls 10%, the position loses $250, which is half of your margin. Liquidation is roughly where the price has fallen about 19.5%, because 1 divided by 5 is 20%, less a 0.5% maintenance margin. The same trade at 20x has a cushion of only about 4.5%.

    Common beginner mistakes here

    • Choosing leverage by how much you could win.
    • Not checking the liquidation price before opening the trade.
    • Adding margin to a losing position instead of closing it.

    What to remember

    • Leverage lets you control a bigger position with a smaller margin. It magnifies profits and losses equally.
    • Liquidation happens when losses use up your margin: roughly a drop of 100 ÷ leverage percent for a long position.
    • Beginners should avoid leverage on real money, and practise at low leverage in paper trading only.
    Try it: In paper trading, open one small position at 2x and note the liquidation price shown. Then compare it to the same trade at 20x. How much room does each give you?

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. You use 10x leverage and the price moves 10% against you. Roughly what happens to your margin?

    At 10x, a 10% adverse move equals about a 100% loss on your margin, so you are close to or at liquidation.

    2. Which has a liquidation price closer to your entry?

    Higher leverage means a smaller adverse move wipes you out, so the liquidation price is closer to your entry.

    3. What is liquidation?

    Liquidation is a forced closure by the exchange to protect the money it lent you. You lose the margin.

    Leverage is a risk amplifier. Learn the basics unlevered, and test low leverage only in paper trading until you have a proven process.
