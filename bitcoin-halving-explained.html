The Bitcoin halving explained: what changes, what doesn't, and the dates

FUNDAMENTALS · 6 MIN READ

Every few years the Bitcoin halving becomes a headline, usually paired with a confident claim about what price will do next. The event itself is simple, scheduled and entirely predictable. The price claims are neither. Here is the mechanism, the full history, and an honest account of what it does and does not tell you.

How new bitcoin is created

Bitcoin transactions are grouped into blocks, and roughly every ten minutes the network adds a new block. The miner who produces it is paid in two ways: transaction fees from the users whose transactions were included, and a fixed amount of newly created bitcoin called the block subsidy or block reward.

That subsidy is the only way new bitcoin enters circulation. When Bitcoin launched in 2009 the subsidy was 50 BTC per block.

What a halving does

The protocol cuts the subsidy in half after every 210,000 blocks. At about ten minutes per block, that works out to roughly four years, although the exact date depends on how quickly blocks are actually found. The schedule is written into Bitcoin's rules, so everyone knows the approximate timing years in advance and nobody can change it without the network agreeing to a rule change.

FirstNovember 201225 BTC
SecondJuly 201612.5 BTC
ThirdMay 20206.25 BTC
FourthApril 20243.125 BTC

The next halving is expected around 2028, which would reduce the subsidy to 1.5625 BTC. You can confirm the current block height and expected timing on any public block explorer.

The 21 million cap

Because the subsidy keeps halving, the total number of bitcoin that will ever exist is capped. Add up the geometric series (210,000 blocks at 50, then at 25, and so on) and the total approaches 21 million. More than 90% of that supply has already been mined. The last fractions will take well over a century to appear because the subsidy shrinks toward zero.

This is the origin of the phrase "digital scarcity." Unlike a currency whose supply a central bank can expand, Bitcoin's issuance path is fixed and public. That predictability is a genuine feature of the design.

What changes for miners

A halving cuts miners' subsidy income in half overnight, measured in bitcoin. Whether that cuts their revenue in half depends on what happens to price and fees at the same time. Miners with high electricity costs or older hardware can become unprofitable and switch off, while more efficient operations carry on. Over time, transaction fees are expected to make up a larger share of miner income as the subsidy declines.

What does not change

Contrary to a common impression, a halving does not change how Bitcoin works day to day. Blocks still arrive about every ten minutes. Transactions confirm in the same way. The network adjusts the mining difficulty about every two weeks, which keeps block times steady even when miners join or leave.

The part about price

The popular story is that fewer new coins mean a higher price, because supply growth slows. The reasoning sounds neat, but it has two problems.

  • It is widely known. A scheduled, public event is something markets can anticipate long before it happens, so any effect may already be reflected in price well before the date.
  • Four events is a tiny sample. Price did rise over the periods after past halvings, but those periods also contained other things: changes in interest rates, regulation, new products, and broader risk appetite. With so few observations, it is impossible to separate the halving from everything else that happened.

Anyone telling you with confidence what a halving will do to price is guessing. We explain why we refuse to make those guesses in why CryptoBolt doesn't predict crypto prices.

How to use this knowledge sensibly

The halving is a useful fact for understanding Bitcoin's monetary design and miner economics. It is not a trading signal. If you follow the market around such events, the more practical tools are the ones that measure what is happening now: volatility, the funding rate, and sentiment gauges like the Fear & Greed index. Periods around big narratives tend to bring larger swings, so it is worth knowing your liquidation price before you use leverage, not after.
