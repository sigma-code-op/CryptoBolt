    The real cost of a trade: fees, spread and slippage

    By the end of this lesson: Calculate the full cost of a round-trip trade and understand why overtrading quietly drains accounts.

    "Trading is free!" is a common claim. It is not. Every trade has costs, and they are charged whether you win or lose. If you ignore them, a strategy that looks fine on paper can lose money in practice.

    The three costs of every trade

    CostWhat it isWho you pay
    FeeA percentage of your trade value, charged on entry and again on exitThe exchange
    SpreadThe gap between the best bid and best ask. Trading instantly means paying itThe other side of your trade
    SlippageGetting a worse price because your order was bigger than what was waiting at the best priceMarket depth

    On a liquid pair, spread and slippage are tiny for small orders. On a small coin they can be several percent, which means you start every trade already behind.

    The round trip

    A round trip is one full trade: buy, then sell. You pay fees on both legs. If your fee is 0.1% per side, a round trip costs about 0.2% before you even count the spread. Fees differ by exchange and account level, so check your own fee table rather than trusting a number from a blog.

    Example: You trade $1,000. Fee is 0.1% per side, so $1 to buy and about $1 to sell. Add a 0.05% spread. Your total cost is roughly $2.50 per round trip. The price has to rise about 0.25% before you merely break even.

    Why frequent trading is expensive

    Costs repeat on every trade. A trader who makes 10 round trips a day at 0.25% each pays about 2.5% of their position size per day in friction alone. Over a month of weekdays that is more than 50% of the position size, and the trader has to be consistently skilled just to cover it. This is a main reason short-term trading is so hard for beginners.

    Key idea: Fewer, better-planned trades beat many impulsive ones, partly because each trade has a fixed price tag. Before you click, ask: "Is the expected move bigger than the cost?"

    Costs you only meet later

    • Funding fees on perpetual futures, paid between longs and shorts every few hours. See funding rates explained.
    • Withdrawal and network fees when you move coins out of an exchange.
    • Taxes in many countries on profits. Rules vary, so ask a qualified local professional.

    To practise the arithmetic on a real position, try our profit and loss guide and the ROI calculator, which both include fees.

    What to remember

    • Every trade costs fees, spread and slippage. You pay them win or lose.
    • A round trip pays fees twice, so your price must move enough just to break even.
    • Overtrading multiplies costs; fewer, planned trades protect your account.

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. Which of these is NOT a normal trading cost?

    Fees, spread and slippage are real costs. No exchange pays a guaranteed profit bonus.

    2. A round trip with 0.1% fee per side costs about how much in fees alone?

    You pay once to buy and once to sell, so fees roughly double to 0.2%.

    3. Why does trading very often make it harder to profit?

    Each round trip has a cost. The more trades you make, the more your skill must exceed the accumulated friction.

    4. You are trading a thin, little-known coin with a 2% spread. What does that mean for a market order?

    A wide spread means the instant buy and sell prices are far apart, so you begin in a hole before the market moves at all.
