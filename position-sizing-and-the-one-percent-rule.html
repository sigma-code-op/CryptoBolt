    Position sizing and the 1% rule: how big should a crypto trade be?

    RISK · 6 MIN READ

    Most beginners decide how big a trade should be by asking how much they want to make. Experienced traders ask the opposite question: how much am I prepared to lose if this is wrong? That one change of question is the core of position sizing, and it matters more to long-term survival than almost any entry technique.

    Size comes from risk, not from conviction

    Your position size is not a measure of how sure you are. It is the answer to a small piece of arithmetic that starts from three numbers you choose before entering:

    • Account size. The money you are trading with.
    • Risk per trade. The share of the account you accept losing if the stop is hit.
    • Stop distance. The gap between your entry and your stop-loss.

    The formula follows directly:

    Position size = (account size × risk per trade) ÷ stop distance per unit

    Example: Your account is $5,000 and you risk 1% per trade, which is $50. You want to buy a coin at $100 with a stop at $95. The stop distance is $5 per coin. Position size = $50 ÷ $5 = 10 coins, a position worth $1,000. If the stop is hit, you lose about $50, which is 1% of the account. If you had wanted a stop at $90 instead, the distance would be $10 and the size would drop to 5 coins. Same risk, smaller position.

    Notice what is missing: leverage never appeared. Risk is set by the size of the loss if you are wrong, and that is determined by the position and the stop, not by how you fund it.

    Why 1% is a common starting point

    There is nothing magical about 1%, but the logic behind small percentages is solid. Consider how many consecutive losses would hurt you at different risk levels.

    Risk per tradeAccount after 10 losses in a rowGain needed to get back
    1%about 90.4% of the startabout 10.6%
    2%about 81.7% of the startabout 22.4%
    5%about 59.9% of the startabout 67%
    10%about 34.9% of the startabout 187%

    Losses compound against you, and recovering is harder than it looks: a 50% drawdown requires a 100% gain just to break even. A small fixed percentage keeps a bad run survivable. Even strategies that win most of the time experience losing streaks, which is why win rate alone says little about safety. Our article on realistic day-trading returns covers what research found about how rarely short-term traders stay ahead.

    Where leverage fits

    Leverage does not change how much you risk if your sizing is right. It changes how much margin you have to post to hold the position, and it moves your liquidation price closer or further away.

    In the example above, the $1,000 position could be held with $1,000 of margin (1x) or $100 (10x). The loss at the stop is the same $50 either way. What differs is the cushion: at 10x the liquidation price is much closer to entry, and if it sits before your stop, the exchange will close you out first and the loss will be your full margin instead of $50. So the rule is simple: size by stop distance, and then verify that the liquidation price lies safely beyond the stop. The position size calculator and the liquidation price calculator work through both steps.

    Common mistakes

    • Moving the stop to fit a bigger position. The stop should come from the trade's logic. If you widen it to keep your size, you have raised your real risk.
    • Sizing to a round dollar amount. "I always buy $1,000" means your risk changes with every stop distance. Risk, not notional, is the thing to keep constant.
    • Ignoring costs. Fees and slippage mean the real loss at a stop is a little more than the plan. Leave some margin of safety.
    • Treating several trades as independent. Three long positions in highly correlated coins behave like one larger position when the market falls together.

    Using risk-reward

    Once size is set by risk, you can evaluate whether a trade is worth taking by its risk-reward ratio: how far to your target compared to how far to your stop. A trade risking $50 to make $150 needs to win far less often to be worthwhile than one risking $50 to make $50. Sizing and risk-reward are two halves of the same decision.

    Practising it

    Position sizing is a habit more than a calculation. Doing it every time, on every trade, even in simulation, is how it becomes automatic.
