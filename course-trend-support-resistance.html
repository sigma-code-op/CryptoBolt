    Simple analysis: trend, support and resistance

    By the end of this lesson: Identify the direction of the trend, mark the key price zones, and use them to plan an entry, a stop and a target.

    Technical analysis means studying past prices to make better decisions. Done sensibly, it does not predict the future. It helps you find places where a trade has a clear stop and a good reward-to-risk. You only need three simple tools to start.

    Tool 1: the trend

    A trend is the general direction price is moving.

    • Uptrend: a series of higher highs and higher lows. Each rally goes further than the last, and each dip stops higher.
    • Downtrend: lower highs and lower lows.
    • Range (sideways): price bounces between a ceiling and a floor without making progress.

    The saying "the trend is your friend" is popular because trades in the direction of the larger trend tend to be easier. As a beginner, check the daily chart first. Is it going up, down or sideways? Then look at the 1-hour chart for your entry. Avoid fighting a strong trend because a coin "looks too high" or "looks too low".

    Tool 2: support and resistance zones

    Support is a price area where falling prices have often stopped and bounced, because buyers stepped in. Resistance is an area where rising prices have often stalled, because sellers appeared. Find them by looking left on the chart for places where price turned several times.

    • They are zones, not exact lines. Draw a band, not a hairline.
    • The more times a zone has held, the more people watch it, but also the likelier it will eventually break.
    • When support breaks, it often becomes resistance, and the reverse.
    • Round numbers like $50,000 often act as zones. See why.
    Example: A coin has bounced off $90 three times and stalled at $110 twice. A beginner plan: buy near $91 with a stop just below $88, and a target near $108. Risk is about $3, reward about $17, so R:R is better than 1:5. The setup has a natural stop (support breaking) and a natural target (resistance). If $88 breaks, the idea is wrong, so you exit small.

    Tool 3: a moving average

    A moving average (MA) is the average closing price over the last N candles, redrawn on every new candle. It smooths out noise so the direction is easier to see. A common beginner choice is the 50-period MA: price above a rising 50-MA is a healthy uptrend; price below a falling one is a downtrend. It lags, meaning it shows what has already happened.

    Adding volume as context

    A move through a zone on strong volume is more meaningful than one on weak volume. A bounce off support on rising volume suggests real buyers. Treat volume as a clue, not a rule.

    The honest limits of analysis

    • Every tool here describes the past. None predicts the future.
    • Smart people look at the same chart and disagree. If someone says a pattern "always works", they are guessing or selling something.
    • More indicators do not equal better decisions. Three clean tools beat fifteen cluttered ones.
    • News, big sellers and sudden events can ignore every line you draw. That is exactly why you use a stop and size by risk.

    Read why we do not predict prices for the longer version.

    Four questions before any entry

    1. What is the trend on the daily chart?
    2. Where are the nearest support and resistance zones?
    3. Where is my stop (the price that proves me wrong), and how big does that make my position?
    4. Is the reward at least about twice the risk?

    Scenario: a range and what breaks it

    Price has moved between $90 and $100 for two weeks and bounced off $90 three times. The $90 area is support because buyers have repeatedly stepped in there. If a candle closes at $88 on strong volume, the idea that buyers will defend $90 is wrong, and your plan should already say what you do then. Levels are places where decisions get made, not guarantees.

    Common beginner mistakes here

    • Drawing a dozen levels on the chart until every price is near one.
    • Treating a wick through a level as a break without waiting for a close.
    • Entering only because price is at a level with no plan for being wrong.

    What to remember

    • An uptrend is higher highs and higher lows; a downtrend is lower highs and lower lows; otherwise price is ranging.
    • Support and resistance are zones where price has turned before. They give natural places for stops and targets.
    • Indicators describe the past. Use a few simple tools, and rely on stops and sizing for the times they are wrong.
    Try it: Pick a pair on the daily chart in the terminal. Identify the trend, then draw two support and two resistance zones. Write down one possible long and one possible short, each with entry, stop and target.

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. Which describes an uptrend?

    An uptrend means each rally and each dip are higher than the last.

    2. Why are support and resistance drawn as zones instead of exact lines?

    Buyers and sellers act around levels, not at a single price, so a band is more realistic.

    3. What does a moving average mainly do?

    A moving average averages recent closes to reduce noise. It lags and does not predict.

    The support was the reason for the trade. When it fails, your plan says you are wrong, and you take the small planned loss.
