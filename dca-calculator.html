    What dollar-cost averaging means

    Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) is the habit of investing the same amount of money at regular intervals, whatever the price happens to be. Instead of deciding when to buy, you decide how much and how often: $200 every two weeks, $50 every Friday, 100 USDT on the first of the month. When the price is low, the fixed amount buys more coins; when it is high, it buys fewer. Over time your average cost per coin lands somewhere between the highs and the lows you paid.

    How this calculator works

    You enter the amount per buy, how many buys you will make, and the price at the first and last buy. The calculator spreads the buys evenly and moves the price in a straight line between those two points. It then adds up the coins each buy would have purchased:

    • Coins bought at each step = amount per buy ÷ price at that step
    • Total invested = amount per buy × number of buys
    • Average cost = total invested ÷ total coins (this is a harmonic average, which is why it sits below the simple average of the prices when prices vary)
    • Final value = total coins × the last price
    • Lump-sum comparison = the same total invested at the first price on day one, valued at the last price

    Worked example: a falling market

    Buy $200 of Bitcoin ten times while the price slides from $50,000 to $40,000. You invest $2,000 in total and end up with about 0.0447 BTC at an average cost of about $44,773 — lower than the $50,000 starting price. At the final price of $40,000 the holding is worth about $1,786.80, a loss of roughly 10.7%. Someone who put all $2,000 in at $50,000 on day one would hold $1,600, a loss of 20%. DCA lost money here too, but about half as much, because most of the money was invested after the price had already fallen.

    Worked example: a rising market

    Now buy $100 of Bitcoin twelve times while the price climbs from $40,000 to $60,000. You invest $1,200 and finish with roughly 0.0244 BTC at an average cost near $49,202, worth about $1,463 at the end — a gain of about 21.9%. The lump sum bought at $40,000 on day one would be worth $1,800, a gain of 50%. DCA lagged because part of your money bought after the price had already risen.

    What the two examples teach

    • In a steadily falling market, DCA loses less than a lump sum; in a steadily rising market it earns less. Nobody knows in advance which one is coming.
    • DCA does not remove risk. It spreads the timing risk of a single entry across many entries, so no single bad day decides your result.
    • The main benefit is behavioural: a fixed schedule removes the urge to wait for the perfect entry or to buy in a panic after a spike.
    • Over long periods, a lump sum has historically beaten DCA more often than not in assets that trend up, simply because money is invested for longer. If you already hold the cash, that trade-off is real.

    Choosing an interval and an amount

    The interval matters less than consistency. Weekly, fortnightly and monthly schedules tend to give similar average costs over a long period; what changes is the number of transactions and therefore the fees. If each purchase costs a fixed fee, many small buys can cost more than they are worth, so check your exchange's fees before choosing a very small amount. Only commit money you will not need soon: a plan you abandon in a downturn forfeits the benefit.

    What this model leaves out

    Real prices do not move in straight lines, so your true average cost will differ. Trading fees, spreads, taxes and any rewards earned while holding are not included. Use the average price calculator to enter your actual buys, and the crypto ROI calculator to include fees. For a fuller discussion read dollar-cost averaging in crypto.

    FAQ

    Crypto DCA calculator FAQ

    What is a DCA calculator?

    A DCA calculator shows what regular, fixed-size purchases would have cost on average and what they would be worth at a later price. This one also compares the result with investing the same total money in one go on the first day.

    Is dollar-cost averaging better than a lump sum?

    Neither wins every time. In a falling or choppy market DCA usually loses less; in a steadily rising market a lump sum usually earns more because the money is invested for longer. DCA is mostly a way to manage the risk of buying at a bad moment and to build a habit.

    Does DCA guarantee a profit?

    No. If the price ends below your average cost you have a loss, as the falling-market example shows. DCA lowers timing risk; it does not remove market risk.

    How often should I buy when dollar-cost averaging?

    Pick an interval you can keep: weekly, fortnightly or monthly are the usual choices. Over long periods the interval changes the average cost less than consistency does, but very frequent buys can add up in fees.

    Why does my real average price differ from this result?

    This calculator uses a straight-line price path. Real prices rise and fall in between, so your actual average cost depends on the exact prices you paid. Enter them in the average price calculator for the precise figure.

    Are fees and taxes included?

    No. Add your exchange fees with the crypto ROI calculator, and check the tax rules where you live, because they vary and can change the real result.
