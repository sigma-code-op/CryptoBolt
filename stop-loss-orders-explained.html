    Stop-loss orders explained: stop-market, stop-limit and why they slip

    MECHANICS · 6 MIN READ

    A stop-loss is the most common risk tool in trading, and one of the most misunderstood. Many people assume it guarantees an exit at a chosen price. It does not. What it guarantees depends on which kind you use, and understanding the difference is worth more than any particular stop level.

    What a stop-loss is

    A stop-loss is an instruction that stays dormant until price reaches a level you set, called the trigger or stop price. At that moment it turns into a real order and tries to close your position. Until the trigger is reached, it is not in the order book at all, which means nobody can see it.

    The key question is what kind of order it becomes when triggered.

    Stop-market versus stop-limit

    Stop-marketStop-limit
    BecomesA market orderA limit order
    GuaranteesThat you get out, if there is anyone to trade withThe worst price you will accept
    Does not guaranteeThe exit priceThat you get out at all
    Main riskSlippage in fast or thin marketsPrice jumps past your limit and the order never fills
    FeesTakerOften maker if it rests, taker if it fills immediately

    A stop-market prioritises certainty of exit over price. A stop-limit prioritises price over certainty. Neither is better in general. Which one fits depends on what you are protecting against.

    Example: You are long at $100 and want to be out if price falls to $95. With a stop-market at $95, a sudden drop that gaps through to $93 fills you around $93 or slightly lower. With a stop-limit with a trigger of $95 and a limit of $94.50, the same drop leaves your order unfilled at $93 because the price already passed your limit. You are still holding the position, now at a larger loss, and the order is sitting at $94.50 waiting for a bounce.

    Why stops slip

    Slippage on a stop is the same mechanical effect described in our piece on reading the order book: a market order fills against whatever is resting, and if size runs out at the best price it moves to the next one. A stop is more likely to suffer for two reasons.

    • Stops cluster. Many traders place them at obvious levels, often just beyond a round number or a recent low. When price reaches that zone, many stop-market orders fire together and all demand liquidity at the same moment.
    • The moment a stop triggers is usually the moment markets are moving fastest, which is when liquidity is thinnest.

    None of this is a reason to avoid stops. It is a reason to expect that your real exit may be worse than the number you typed in, and to size your position with that in mind.

    Wicks and the trigger price

    Many exchanges let you choose what triggers the stop: the last traded price or the mark price. A brief spike on a single venue, a wick, can touch a last-price trigger and stop you out even though the market closes the candle back above your level. A mark-price trigger, which is derived from an index across venues, is less sensitive to that kind of single-exchange spike. Check which option your platform uses, because the default is not always the one you would choose.

    On futures: stops and liquidation

    If you trade with leverage, the stop and the liquidation price interact. A stop that sits beyond your liquidation price is useless, because the exchange will close the position first. Work out your liquidation level before you pick a stop, and keep the stop comfortably inside it. The liquidation price calculator can do that arithmetic for you.

    Placing a stop with a reason

    The most common mistake is choosing a stop by feel or by a percentage that sounds comfortable. A stop works better when it is tied to something about the trade:

    • Invalidation. Place it at the level where your reason for entering is clearly wrong, not at a random distance.
    • Volatility. A stop tighter than the asset's normal movement will be hit by noise. Measures such as ATR express typical movement in price terms and are one way to set a distance that fits the coin you are trading.
    • Size. Once you know the stop distance, work backwards to the position size that makes the loss acceptable. That calculation is covered in our guide to position sizing.

    Practice before it costs money

    How a stop behaves in a fast market is hard to appreciate from a description. Using paper trading on live prices lets you see slippage and stop-outs happen with virtual funds, which is far cheaper than learning it with real ones.
