    Stops and risk-reward: plan the exit before the entry

    By the end of this lesson: Plan every trade with an entry, a stop and a target, and calculate whether the trade is worth taking before you place it.

    A professional never enters a trade without three numbers already written down: where they get in, where they admit they are wrong, and where they will take profit. Beginners often have only the first.

    The stop: where your idea is proven wrong

    A stop-loss is the price at which you close a losing trade. Good stops are not random distances like "5%". They go at a level where, if price reaches it, your reason for the trade no longer holds.

    • Long trade: place the stop just below a recent low or a support level. If price breaks that, the buyers you were counting on have lost.
    • Short trade: place it just above a recent high or resistance.

    Give a little breathing room. Price often pokes past an obvious level before turning, which is called a stop hunt or just noise. A stop exactly on a round number is easy to hit; a bit beyond is usually wiser. Our article on round-number levels explains why.

    The golden rule of stops: You may move a stop in the direction of safety (to lock in profit). You must never move it further away to avoid taking a loss. That is how small losses become big ones.

    The target and risk-reward

    The target is where you plan to take profit. Compare it to your stop to get the risk-reward ratio:

    Risk-reward (R:R) = potential profit ÷ potential loss.
    Entry $100, stop $95 (risk $5), target $115 (reward $15) → R:R = 15 ÷ 5 = 3, written 1:3.

    Traders call the risk amount 1R. A trade that makes three times the risk is a +3R win, and a stopped-out trade is −1R. Counting results in R, not in dollars, lets you compare trades of any size. See R-multiple.

    Why you do not need to win most trades

    Here is the idea that surprises beginners: with a good reward-to-risk, you can lose more often than you win and still come out ahead. The break-even win rate is:

    Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R:R)
    Risk-rewardWin rate needed just to break even
    1:150%
    1:233.3%
    1:325%
    1:0.5 (risking $2 to make $1)66.7%

    The last row is a warning: if you risk more than you stand to gain, you must be right most of the time just to break even. Many beginners do exactly this by taking quick small profits and giving big losses room to run.

    Expectancy turns this into one number: the average result per trade, in R.

    Example: You win 40% of trades with a 1:2 ratio. Expectancy = 0.40 × 2 − 0.60 × 1 = 0.8 − 0.6 = +0.2R per trade. Over 100 trades at 1% risk, that averages about +20R, roughly +20% before costs. With a win rate of 30% and the same ratio it would be 0.3 × 2 − 0.7 = −0.1R, a slow loss. See expectancy and our guide to risk-reward and win rate.

    These figures are examples to show the maths. They are not forecasts, and real results will vary because costs and slippage reduce them.

    The three-number checklist

    1. Entry: at what price do I get in?
    2. Stop: at what price am I wrong?
    3. Target: where do I take profit, and is the reward at least about twice the risk?

    If you cannot fill in all three, you are not ready to place the trade. Skip it. There will be another one tomorrow.

    What to remember

    • Put the stop where your trade idea is proven wrong, and never move it farther away to dodge a loss.
    • Risk-reward = reward ÷ risk. Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R:R), so a 1:2 plan only needs to win about 34% of the time to break even.
    • Every trade needs an entry, a stop and a target written down before you click.
    Try it: Choose any pair in the terminal. On the 4h chart, pick a long entry, put a stop under the last low and a target at the next high. Calculate the R:R above. If it is under 1:2, would you still take it?

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. Entry $200, stop $190, target $230. What is the risk-reward ratio?

    Risk = $10. Reward = $30. Reward ÷ risk = 3, so 1:3.

    2. With a 1:3 risk-reward, roughly what win rate do you need to break even (before costs)?

    Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + 3) = 25%.

    3. Price is falling toward your stop. What should you do?

    Moving a stop away from price turns a planned small loss into an unplanned large one. Keep the plan.

    4. You win 50% of trades but each win is 0.5R and each loss is 1R. What is your expectancy per trade?

    0.5 × 0.5 − 0.5 × 1 = 0.25 − 0.5 = −0.25R. Winning half the time is not enough when your wins are small.
