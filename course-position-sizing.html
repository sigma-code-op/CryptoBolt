    How much to risk: position sizing

    By the end of this lesson: Work out the exact size of a trade from your account, your risk limit and your stop, instead of guessing.

    New traders spend 95% of their effort on when to buy and almost none on how much to buy. Professionals do the opposite, because size is the one thing you fully control. You cannot control where price goes. You can control how much a wrong guess costs you.

    Risk is not the same as position size

    These are different numbers, and mixing them up is the root of most blown accounts.

    • Position size is how much you buy, for example $500 worth of a coin.
    • Risk is how much you lose if you are wrong, meaning if price hits your stop.

    A $500 position with a stop 2% away risks $10. A $500 position with a stop 20% away risks $100. Same size, ten times the risk. This is why you must know your stop before you decide the size.

    The 1% rule

    A widely used guideline is to risk no more than 1% of your account on any single trade (some use 0.5% to 2%). On a $1,000 account, that is $10 of risk per trade. It sounds tiny. That is the point.

    Position size formula
    Size (in coins) = (account × risk %) ÷ (entry price − stop price)
    Position value = size × entry price
    Example: Account $1,000, risk 1% = $10. You want to buy at $100 with a stop at $95. The risk per coin is $5. So size = $10 ÷ $5 = 2 coins, a $200 position. If the stop is hit you lose about $10, which is 1% of the account. If you had wanted a tighter stop at $98, the risk per coin would be $2, and the size could be 5 coins ($500).

    Why risk such a small slice?

    Because losing streaks are normal, and losses are harder to recover from than they look. First, a streak: if you lose 10 trades in a row, which happens to everyone, risking 1% each leaves you down about 10%. Risking 10% each leaves you down about 65%.

    Risk per tradeAccount after 10 losses in a row
    1%about 90.4% left (down 9.6%)
    2%about 81.7% left
    5%about 59.9% left
    10%about 34.9% left (down 65%)

    Second, the maths of recovery works against you:

    Loss from peakGain needed to get back
    10%11.1%
    25%33.3%
    50%100%
    75%300%

    A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to repair. Deep drawdowns are where accounts die. Small, fixed risk keeps you in the game long enough to improve. You can test how likely a rough patch is with our losing streak calculator.

    Rules to take away

    1. Decide your stop first, based on the chart. Never choose it based on how much you want to risk.
    2. Then calculate size from the stop distance. A wider stop means a smaller position.
    3. Do not increase size after wins out of excitement or after losses to "win it back".
    4. If the calculated size is too small to be worth it, skip the trade. That is a feature, not a bug.

    A full sizing walkthrough

    Suppose your account is $2,000 and you decide to risk 1% per trade, which is $20. You want to buy a coin at $50 with a stop at $47. The distance between them is $3 per coin.

    Position size is the amount you risk divided by the risk per unit: $20 ÷ $3 is about 6.67 coins. That position costs about $333 at $50. If the stop is hit, you lose about $20, plus a little for fees and slippage. If you had instead bought 20 coins because it "felt right", the same stop would cost $60, three times your plan.

    Notice what drove the size: not your confidence, but the stop distance. A wider stop means fewer coins. A tighter stop means more coins, although tighter stops are hit more often by ordinary noise. The calculation forces a trade-off you would otherwise skip.

    Key idea: Size = (account × risk %) ÷ (entry − stop). Fix the percentage, find the stop from the chart, and let the position size be the result.

    Why small risk per trade matters so much

    Losses are harder to recover than they look. A 10% loss needs about an 11% gain to get back to even. A 25% loss needs about 33%. A 50% loss needs 100%. Because of this asymmetry, a string of losses at big size can leave an account in a hole that is very hard to climb out of, even if your strategy is decent.

    Consider ten losing trades in a row, which happens more often than people expect for strategies that win less than half the time. At 1% risk each, you are down about 9.6%. At 5% risk each, you are down about 40%. At 10% risk each, you are down about 65%. The strategy is the same, the outcome is entirely different, and only the sizing changed.

    Adjusting for real conditions

    • Volatility. If a coin routinely swings 8% a day, a 2% stop will be hit constantly. Either use a wider stop with a smaller size, or skip the coin.
    • Several positions at once. If three trades all depend on the same market move, they are really one large trade. Count their combined risk, not each one alone.
    • Fees and slippage. Allow a little extra, so a stop-out that costs slightly more than planned does not break your limit.
    • Account changes. Recalculate from the current balance now and then. Do not keep sizing from an old, larger number after losses.

    A routine you can repeat every time

    1. Pick the risk percentage for the account and keep it fixed while you learn.
    2. Find the exit that proves your idea wrong, based on the chart rather than the money.
    3. Run the numbers in the {{position-size-calculator|position size calculator}}.
    4. Check the liquidation price if you use leverage. It should sit beyond your stop.
    5. Write the plan down: entry, stop, size, and the dollar loss if wrong.

    It takes a minute, and it is the single habit that most often separates accounts that survive from accounts that do not.

    Scenario: Ana sizes her first trade

    Ana has $2,000 and decides to risk 1%, which is $20. Her entry is $50 and her stop is $48, so the stop distance is $2. She can buy 10 coins, a $500 position. If the stop is hit she loses about $20. If she had wanted to buy 40 coins "because it feels right", the same stop would cost $80, or 4% of her account.

    Common beginner mistakes here

    • Letting confidence decide the size.
    • Putting the stop where the position size works instead of where the chart says the idea is wrong.
    • Risking a bigger percentage after a win.

    What to remember

    • Risk is the amount you lose if you are wrong, not the size of the position. Set the stop first, then calculate size.
    • Size = (account × risk %) ÷ (entry − stop). Many traders cap risk at about 1% per trade.
    • Big losses are disproportionately hard to recover from: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain.
    Try it: In the calculator above, enter your paper-trading balance, a 1% risk, and a stop 3% below your entry. Write down the size. Then widen the stop to 6% and see what happens to the size.

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. Account $2,000, risk 1%, entry $50, stop $48. What position size (in coins) does the formula give?

    Risk = $20. Risk per coin = $50 − $48 = $2. Size = $20 ÷ $2 = 10 coins.

    2. What happens to position size if you place your stop farther away, keeping the same risk %?

    A wider stop means a bigger loss per coin, so to keep the same dollar risk you must buy fewer coins.

    3. How much gain is needed to recover from a 50% loss?

    If $1,000 becomes $500, you need to double $500 to get back to $1,000, which is a 100% gain.

    4. Which is the better order of decisions?

    The stop comes from the chart and your plan. The size is then calculated so that hitting it costs only your chosen risk.
