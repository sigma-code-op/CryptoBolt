    Reading a price chart: candles and timeframes

    By the end of this lesson: Read any single candle at a glance and choose a sensible timeframe for what you want to do.

    A price chart looks scary, but it is only a record of what the price did. The most common chart type uses candlesticks. Learn to read one candle and you can read the whole chart.

    One candle shows four prices

    Each candle covers a fixed slice of time, for example one hour, and records four numbers:

    • Open: the price at the start of that period.
    • High: the highest price reached during it.
    • Low: the lowest price reached during it.
    • Close: the price at the end of the period.

    The thick part is the body. It runs from open to close. The thin lines above and below are the wicks (also called shadows). They show how far price went before being pushed back.

    Colour rule: On most charts a candle is green if it closed higher than it opened, and red if it closed lower. Some platforms let you swap the colours, so always check the settings before you trust your eyes.

    Build one yourself

    Change the four numbers and watch the candle redraw. Try making a candle with a very long upper wick, then one with almost no wicks at all.

    What a candle can and cannot tell you

    What you seeWhat it describes
    Long green body, tiny wicksBuyers were in control for the whole period and price closed near the high.
    Long red body, tiny wicksSellers were in control for the whole period.
    Long upper wickPrice pushed up, then sellers drove it back down before the close. The move up was rejected.
    Long lower wickPrice dropped, then buyers pushed it back up. The move down was rejected.
    Small body, wicks both sidesA tug of war with no clear winner. Often called indecision.

    Notice the wording: these describe what already happened. A candle is a summary of the past, not a promise about the next one. Plenty of beginners memorise candle "patterns" with funny names and believe they predict the future. They do not, at least not reliably.

    Timeframes: the same market, different stories

    A timeframe is how much time each candle covers: 1 minute, 15 minutes, 1 hour, 4 hours, 1 day, 1 week. The same market can look like a crash on the 1-minute chart and a gentle pullback on the daily chart.

    TimeframeGood forWatch out for
    1m to 5mVery short-term tradingLots of noise, fast decisions, high costs
    15m to 1hDay trading entriesEasy to overtrade
    4h to 1DSeeing the bigger picture, swing tradesSlower, so patience matters
    1WLong-term trendFew signals

    For a beginner, start on the 1-hour to 1-day charts. They move slower, give you time to think, and cost less in fees because you trade less. Then zoom in to a smaller timeframe only to fine-tune an entry you already decided on.

    Volume: how many people took part

    Under the candles you will usually see bars showing volume, the amount traded in that period. A big move on high volume means many participants agreed. A big move on thin volume is easier to reverse. Volume is context, not a signal on its own.

    Go deeper: every major pattern, with trade rules, risk notes and crypto-specific guidance, is covered in the ebook The Complete Book of Candlestick Patterns (a free sample is on the ebook page).

    What to remember

    • A candle records open, high, low and close for one time period; the body spans open to close and the wicks show the extremes.
    • Candles describe what has already happened. They do not reliably predict what comes next.
    • Beginners should start on 1-hour to 1-day charts; very small timeframes bring noise, stress and higher costs.
    Try it: Open the terminal and view one pair on the 15m, 4h and 1D charts. Take a screenshot of each. Write one sentence on how the story changes as you zoom out.

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. A candle opened at $100, reached a high of $108, a low of $99 and closed at $106. What colour is it on a standard chart?

    Close ($106) is above open ($100), so it is an up candle, normally green.

    2. A candle with a very long upper wick and a small body most likely means:

    The wick shows how far price went. A long upper wick means buyers pushed up and sellers drove it back.

    3. Which timeframe is usually most suitable for a complete beginner to start with?

    Slower timeframes have less noise and give you time to think. They also tend to mean fewer trades and lower fees.

    4. What does a candlestick pattern prove about the next candle?

    Candles summarise the past. Patterns can give context but never certainty.
