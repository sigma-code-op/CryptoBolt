    Order types: market, limit and stop

    By the end of this lesson: Choose between a market, limit and stop order, and know exactly what each one does when you press the button.

    An order is an instruction you give the exchange. The type of order decides how and at what price your trade is carried out. Using the wrong type is one of the cheapest ways for a beginner to lose money by accident.

    First, the waiting list

    Recall the meeting place from lesson 2. Waiting buy orders are called bids and waiting sell orders are called asks. Together they form the order book. The gap between the best bid and the best ask is the spread.

    Remember these two prices, because they decide everything below: you buy at the ask and you sell at the bid, if you want to trade immediately.

    Market order: "do it now"

    A market order says: buy or sell right now at the best available price. It fills immediately, almost always. The catch is that you do not control the exact price. If you are buying, you take the cheapest asks, and if your order is bigger than what is waiting there, you eat into higher and higher prices. That extra cost is called slippage.

    • Pros: fast, certain to fill.
    • Cons: you pay the spread and possible slippage; you can get a worse price than you expected in a fast market.
    • Use it when: getting in or out matters more than a small price difference, for example closing a losing trade quickly.

    Limit order: "only at this price or better"

    A limit order says: buy no higher than this price, or sell no lower than this price. It joins the order book and waits. If the market reaches your price, you are filled. If it never does, you are not filled at all.

    • Pros: you control the price; often lower fees; no slippage.
    • Cons: it may never fill, and you may miss the move.
    • Use it when: you are patient and have a specific price in mind.
    Example: A coin trades at $100. You want to buy, but only if it dips to $98. You place a limit buy at $98. If the price drops to $98, you buy. If it goes up without touching $98, nothing happens and you keep your cash.

    Stop order: "if it goes there, then act"

    A stop order stays dormant until the price reaches a trigger level you set (the stop price). Then it activates. Stops are mostly used to limit a loss: you own a coin bought at $100, and you set a stop at $95. If price falls to $95, your order triggers and sells.

    TypeWhat happens at the triggerRisk
    Stop-marketBecomes a market order and sells at whatever price is availableYou are almost sure to exit, but possibly at a worse price than the stop
    Stop-limitBecomes a limit order at the price you setYou control the price, but in a fast fall the order may never fill, so you stay in the trade

    A stop is a plan, not a guarantee. In a sudden drop the exit price can be lower than your stop. Our deeper guide to stop-loss orders covers this. In lesson 8 you will learn where to put one.

    Maker and taker: why exchanges charge differently

    If your order waits in the book (a limit order), you are a maker: you add liquidity. If your order takes what is already waiting (a market order), you are a taker. Many exchanges charge takers a higher fee than makers. Over many trades, that adds up.

    A simple rule for beginners

    • Entering: use a limit order when you can wait; use a market order only when speed truly matters.
    • Protecting: set a stop as soon as you are in.
    • Always check the order preview before you confirm: side (buy or sell), price, amount and fee.

    What to remember

    • You buy at the ask and sell at the bid when trading instantly; the gap between them is the spread.
    • Market orders are fast but cost the spread and possible slippage; limit orders control the price but may not fill.
    • A stop order triggers at a price you choose to limit losses. It is a plan, not a guarantee of the exit price.
    Try it: In paper trading, place one limit buy order a little below the current price and watch whether it fills. Then place a small market order and compare the price you got to the price you saw on screen.

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. You want to buy a coin only if it falls to $98 (currently $100). Which order fits?

    A limit buy waits at $98 and only fills at that price or lower.

    2. What is slippage?

    Slippage is the cost of a market order eating through several price levels, so you pay or receive a worse average price than expected.

    3. Which statement about a stop-limit order is true?

    A stop-limit becomes a limit order at the trigger, so in a fast move price can skip past your limit and leave you in the trade.

    4. You place a limit order that sits in the order book and later gets filled. Which role were you?

    Orders that wait in the book add liquidity, so you are the maker. Many exchanges charge makers lower fees.
