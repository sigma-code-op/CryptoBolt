    Your trading plan and journal

    By the end of this lesson: Write a one-page trading plan you can follow, and start a journal that turns every trade into a lesson.

    Almost every consistent trader, regardless of style, has two habits that beginners skip: a written plan and a journal. They are boring, which is exactly why they work. A plan stops you improvising in the heat of the moment. A journal shows you what you actually do, as opposed to what you think you do.

    The trading plan

    A plan is a short document that answers: when will I trade, how, and when will I stop? It should fit on one page. If it is longer, you will not follow it.

    SectionQuestion it answers
    MarketsWhich 1 to 3 pairs will I trade? (Fewer is better.)
    TimeframesWhich chart gives the trend, and which gives my entry?
    SetupWhat exact conditions must be true before I enter?
    Entry and order typeHow do I get in (for example, limit order at support)?
    StopWhere is my idea proven wrong?
    TargetWhere do I take profit? Minimum reward-to-risk?
    RiskWhat % of the account per trade? Maximum loss per day and per week?
    No-trade rulesWhen do I stay out (tired, after the daily limit, big news, no clear setup)?

    The trading journal

    A journal is a record of each trade. After 30 trades it becomes a mirror. You will see patterns you cannot see in the moment: perhaps you lose money on Fridays, or every time you trade without a stop, or after two wins in a row.

    Record these for every trade, ideally right after you close it:

    • Date and pair, and long or short.
    • Entry, stop, target and size, planned and actual.
    • Result in R (for example +2R or −1R) and in money.
    • Why you took it, in one sentence, and a screenshot of the chart.
    • How you felt (calm, rushed, bored, excited) on a 1 to 5 scale.
    • Did you follow your plan? Yes or no. Be honest.

    TueSOL/USDT longChased a green candle−1RNoFOMO. Entered without a stop level.

    The example above is made up, to show the format. Notice that the middle row is the one to learn from, and also that a loss that follows the plan (the last row) is a good trade. Judge yourself on process, not on any single result.

    The weekly review

    Once a week, spend 20 minutes with your journal and answer:

    1. How many trades did I take, and what was my total in R?
    2. How many broke my plan? What did those cost me?
    3. What is one thing I will do differently next week?

    Change one thing at a time. Over a month, this review loop will teach you more than any course, including this one.

    Scenario: what 20 journal entries reveal

    After 20 logged trades, a trader has 8 wins averaging +1.8R and 12 losses of -1R. The result is (8 x 1.8 - 12) / 20, or about +0.12R per trade. That is barely above zero, so the journal says the edge, if there is one, is small and costs matter. The same journal might also show that the three worst losses all happened after skipping the plan.

    Common beginner mistakes here

    • Journaling only the winners.
    • Writing results but not the reason for the trade or how you felt.
    • Reviewing too rarely to catch repeating mistakes.

    What to remember

    • A one-page written plan sets your markets, setup, entry, stop, target, risk and no-trade rules in advance.
    • A journal records every trade with the reason, result in R, your mood and whether you followed the plan.
    • Judge trades by process: a planned loss is a good trade, and an impulsive win is a bad one.

    Check your understanding

    Four quick questions. Wrong answers are fine: the explanation is where you learn.

    1. What is the main purpose of a written trading plan?

    A plan lets you act on decisions you made while calm, rather than improvising when stressed.

    2. In a journal, a trade that hit your stop exactly as planned should be marked as:

    Process matters more than a single outcome. A planned loss is part of normal trading.

    3. How often should you do a review of your journal?

    A short weekly review is frequent enough to spot patterns and fix habits.

    4. Which journal field helps find emotional patterns?

    Rating how you felt shows connections between emotions (rushed, bored, angry) and bad decisions.
