    Leverage and liquidation explained

    What actually happens when a leveraged futures position is liquidated.

    • How margin multiplied by leverage sets your position size
    • Roughly how far price must fall to liquidate a long at 2x, 5x, 10x, 20x and 50x
    • Why a stop-loss belongs well inside the liquidation price
    Read the transcript

    Leverage lets you control a bigger position with less money. With $100 of margin at 10x, you control a $1,000 position, so a 10% move against you moves your margin by 100%. Approximate drop to liquidation for a long position, before fees and maintenance margin: about 50% at 2x, 20% at 5x, 10% at 10x, 5% at 20x and 2% at 50x. In the example, price from $100 dips to about $88 and then recovers. The 10x position, with liquidation near $90, is closed out during the dip, while the 2x position is still open. Remember: more leverage means less room before liquidation, place a stop-loss well inside the liquidation price, and fees and funding move the real liquidation price. The numbers are illustrative and this is educational content, not financial advice.

    How to read an order book

    Bids, asks, the spread and slippage, animated.

    • The difference between bids (buyers) and asks (sellers)
    • How the spread is the best ask minus the best bid
    • Why a market order can fill at a worse average price than the one on screen
    Read the transcript

    Asks are sellers' orders waiting to sell, listed above the middle line. Bids are buyers' orders waiting to buy, listed below it. The spread is the best ask minus the best bid; in the example it is 0.01, and a tight spread usually means a liquid market. A market order to buy 10 units takes the cheapest asks first and then moves up the book to more expensive ones, so the average fill price ends up above the best ask. That gap is slippage. A very large bid, a so-called wall, can look like support, but it is only a promise and can be cancelled at any time. Remember: bids are buyers and asks are sellers, a wide spread or thin book means more slippage, and walls are hints, not guarantees. The numbers are illustrative and this is educational content, not financial advice.

    Position sizing and the 1% rule

    How to size a trade so one loss cannot do much damage.

    • How to turn a 1% risk limit into a position size
    • The formula: position size = risk amount divided by stop distance
    • Why compounded losses at 10% risk hurt far more than at 1%
    Read the transcript

    Start from what you are willing to lose. With a $1,000 account and a 1% risk limit, you risk $10 per trade. With an entry at $100 and a stop at $98, the stop distance is 2%, so the position size is $10 divided by 2%, which is $500. If the stop is hit you lose $10, which is 1% of the account. Why small risk matters: after ten losses in a row, risking 1% per trade leaves about $904 of the $1,000, while risking 10% per trade leaves about $349. Remember: decide the risk in dollars first, position size equals risk divided by stop distance, and smaller losses keep you in the game. The numbers are illustrative and this is educational content, not financial advice.

    How paper trading works

    Practice with $10,000 of virtual money before risking real money.

    • How a paper account starts with $10,000 of virtual funds
    • Why to set the stop-loss and take-profit before entering
    • What paper trading cannot teach you: emotion, fees and slippage
    Read the transcript

    Step one: every account starts with $10,000 in virtual funds. Step two: pick a market and trade at live prices. Step three: set your size, stop-loss and take-profit, so the exit is planned before the entry. Step four: review your results and learn from each trade. In the example, a closed trade shows a gain of $21.25 and the balance updates. You can reset the balance any time to test a new idea from a clean slate. Remember: paper results are practice, not a promise, test your plan, stops and sizing first, and real markets add emotion, fees and slippage. The numbers are illustrative and this is educational content, not financial advice.
